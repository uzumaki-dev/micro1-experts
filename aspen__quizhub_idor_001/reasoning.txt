The Aspen pipeline grades a frontier model on its ability to author a hidden
test suite for a real broken-access-control vulnerability — not to fix the
bug, but to codify the threat model that any correct fix would have to
satisfy. The agent reads a small FastAPI quiz/assessment service with a
seeded IDOR-class leak across eight endpoints, identifies the leak surface (a
private answer_bundle reachable by students who did not author the quiz), and
writes a test suite that (a) catches the leak at every serialization site,
(b) catches over-eager fix shapes that would regress legitimate student or
instructor flows, and (c) doesn't over-specify any single fix shape.

Test-authoring is the right shape here because the fix is genuinely
ambiguous: a correct fix can redact the response, scrub the persisted row, or
gate the route at auth. Scoring the agent against a hidden fix conflates
"which fix did you pick" with "did you understand the threat." The test suite
is the unambiguous artifact — it must catch the leak regardless of fix shape
and still preserve the legitimate flows.

The 13 rubric items decompose the threat model into three structural groups:
read-side leak coverage (5 criteria across the public read surface),
write-side laundering chains (3 criteria for the duplicate-then-X workflow
shapes), and anti-overblock regression guards (5 criteria for the
dual-contract reasoning that prevents an over-eager fix from passing).
Severity weights (major=3, minor=2, nitpick=1) reflect the relative impact of
each rubric miss; a perfect suite earns 35/35.
